Cadet Drill Commands Explained

Drill looks baffling from the outside and obvious from the inside. The trick is that almost every command follows the same two-part shape — once you hear it, the rest gets much easier.

Why drill is on the syllabus at all

Drill is not really about marching. It teaches you to listen precisely, react immediately and move as part of a group rather than as an individual — which is why it turns up early in the training syllabus and keeps appearing as you progress through star levels. It is also the fastest way to make a group of strangers look and feel like one squad.

Every command has two parts

This is the thing that makes drill click. Nearly every command is made of a cautionary part and an executive part:

  • Cautionary — tells you what is about to happen and who it applies to. It is drawn out. “Squad…”, “By the left…”
  • Executive — the sharp word that means do it now. “…’shun!”, “…quick march!”

You move on the executive word, never on the cautionary one. Most early mistakes are simply moving too soon — the pause between the two parts is deliberate, and it is there to give everyone time to get ready.

Common static commands

These are given while the squad is standing still:

  • Attention — the position of readiness. Heels together, body still, eyes front.
  • Stand at ease — a formal relaxed position, still silent and still in place.
  • Stand easy — a further relaxation from stand at ease; you may move slightly, but you stay where you are.
  • Right dress / eyes front — used to straighten the ranks and then return to looking forward.
  • Left turn, right turn, about turn — changing which way you face without moving position.

Common marching commands

  • Quick march — begin marching at the standard pace, stepping off with the left foot.
  • Halt — stop, brought in on a specific foot so the whole squad stops together.
  • Mark time — march on the spot without moving forward.
  • Left wheel / right wheel — the squad changes direction while continuing to march.

How to actually remember them

  • Listen for the shape, not the words. If you catch the rhythm of cautionary-then-executive, you will react at the right moment even when you cannot quite make out the word.
  • Learn the foot, not just the order. Several commands are given on a particular foot. Counting your own pace makes halts and turns land together.
  • Practise standing still. It sounds daft, but holding attention properly is what most people find hardest, and it is worth more marks than any fancy movement.
  • Watch the person who is good at it. Copying timing is faster than memorising descriptions.

One important caveat

Exact wording, timings and the finer points of drill vary between counties, contingents and occasions, and your instructors and drill manual are always the authority. This guide is a plain-English orientation, not a drill manual — if what your instructor says differs from what you read here, your instructor is right.

Quick answers

Drill commands: FAQs

What is the difference between a cautionary and an executive command?
The cautionary part tells you what is coming and who it applies to, and is drawn out - for example “Squad...”. The executive part is the sharp word that means move now. You act on the executive word only, never on the cautionary one.
What is the difference between stand at ease and stand easy?
Stand at ease is a formal relaxed position in which you stay still and silent. Stand easy is a further relaxation from that position, allowing slight movement, but you remain in place. Stand easy is always commanded from stand at ease, not from attention.
Why does drill matter if cadets is not about the army?
Drill teaches precise listening, immediate reaction and moving as a group rather than as individuals. Those are general discipline and teamwork skills. Being a cadet does not commit anyone to joining the armed forces.
Do all units use exactly the same commands?
The core commands are consistent, but wording, timing and detail vary between counties, contingents and occasions. Your instructors and your drill manual are the authority for your unit.
