Map Reading for Cadets: Grid References

Grid references are the bit of navigation everyone gets wrong first and then never forgets. Here is the whole thing in plain English, including the mistake that costs the most marks.

What the grid actually is

An Ordnance Survey map is covered in blue squares. Each square is one kilometre across. The vertical lines running up the map are eastings, numbered left to right. The horizontal lines running across are northings, numbered bottom to top.

A grid reference is just a way of naming one of those squares, and then a point inside it.

Eastings first: along the corridor, up the stairs

This is the single rule that matters, and it is the one people get backwards under pressure: you always give the easting before the northing. The standard memory hook is “along the corridor, then up the stairs” — you walk along first, then you go up.

Get the order wrong and you will confidently give a reference to somewhere entirely different, which is exactly why instructors test it so hard.

Four-figure grid references

A four-figure reference identifies a whole one-kilometre square. Take the number of the easting line on the left of your square, then the number of the northing line at the bottom of it.

So a square whose left edge is easting 34 and whose bottom edge is northing 21 is grid reference 3421. Note that you always use the lines below and to the left — the ones the square sits on.

Six-figure grid references

A whole kilometre square is not precise enough to find a bridge or a trig point, so a six-figure reference divides each square mentally into ten by ten, giving you a 100-metre square.

Imagine the square divided into tenths across and tenths up. Estimate how many tenths across your point is, and add that digit after the easting. Do the same going up, and add that digit after the northing.

So a point four tenths across and seven tenths up inside square 3421 becomes 344 217. The easting 34 gains a 4; the northing 21 gains a 7.

The mistakes that cost marks

  • Giving northings first. The most common error by far. Along the corridor, then up the stairs.
  • Using the lines above or right of the square. Always take the line below and the line to the left.
  • Splitting the digits in the wrong place. A six-figure reference is three digits then three digits — 344 217, not 34 42 17.
  • Forgetting the grid letters. Numbers repeat across the country, so a full reference often needs the two-letter square as well. Ask your instructor what applies to the map you are using.
  • Estimating tenths carelessly. A tenth of a kilometre square is 100 metres. Sloppy estimating puts you a field away.

Practising without a map in front of you

Grid references reward repetition more than understanding — once the order is automatic you stop making the mistake under pressure. Quizzing yourself little and often between parade nights works far better than one long session before an assessment, which is the same principle covered in our guide to revising for cadet assessments.

Quick answers

Grid references: FAQs

What is the difference between a four-figure and a six-figure grid reference?
A four-figure reference identifies a one-kilometre square. A six-figure reference divides that square into tenths in each direction, identifying a 100-metre square within it, which is far more precise.
Do eastings or northings come first?
Eastings always come first. The standard memory aid is “along the corridor, then up the stairs” - you go along before you go up. Giving them the wrong way round is the most common mistake in map reading.
Which grid lines do I use for the square?
Always the line below your square and the line to the left of it. Using the lines above or to the right gives a reference to a different square.
How big is one grid square on an OS map?
One kilometre across. A six-figure reference narrows that to roughly a 100-metre square, which is why careful estimating of tenths matters.
